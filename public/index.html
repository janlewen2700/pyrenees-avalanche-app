WINTER TRAVEL EDUCATION

Learn before you commit to terrain

This hub is a practical starting point for understanding bulletins, snow, terrain, stability tests and companion rescue. It is not a replacement for an in-person avalanche course, current official BPA, or professional instruction.

Core idea
More information does not make a slope safe. Use it to make better terrain choices and to preserve margins when uncertainty is high.

A simple daily process

1

Read the official BPA

Note the danger level, elevation bands, aspects, avalanche problems, expected size, likelihood/distribution and whether conditions are rising or falling.

2

Check weather & recent loading

New snow, rain, wind transport and rapid warming can change the problem faster than a static number suggests.

3

Choose terrain that fits

Build a primary route and a lower-consequence Plan B before leaving. Avoid depending on a single test or a single observation.

4

Reassess continuously

Compare what you see with the forecast. Recent avalanches, cracking, whumpfs, rapid loading or warming are reasons to increase your margin.

Before entering avalanche terrain: every person should carry a working transceiver, probe and shovel, know how to use them, and understand the group plan.

Avalanche basics

Slab avalanches

A cohesive slab releases on a weak layer or interface and can propagate far beyond the trigger point. Wind slab, storm slab and persistent/deep-persistent slab problems are different expressions of this mechanism.

Loose avalanches

Loose snow releases start from a point and fan outward. They can be dry or wet; even a relatively small loose avalanche can be serious above cliffs, gullies or other terrain traps.

Wet-snow problems

Rain, sustained warmth or strong solar input can weaken the snowpack. Timing matters: conditions can deteriorate rapidly during the day.

Cornices

Cornices can fail farther back than expected and can trigger slabs below. Give corniced ridgelines a generous margin from both above and below.

Red flags

  • Recent natural or human-triggered avalanches
  • Shooting cracks around skis, board or boots
  • Whumpfing/collapse of a buried weak layer
  • Rapid loading from snowfall or wind
  • Rain on snow or rapid warming

Snowpack: what you are trying to understand

A snowpack is a stack of layers produced by storms, wind, temperature gradients, sun, rain and metamorphism. The useful question is not simply “is the snow hard or soft?” but whether a weak layer exists, whether a cohesive slab sits above it, and whether a fracture could initiate and propagate.

New & wind-loaded snow

Look for recent storm interfaces, density changes and fresh wind slabs near ridgelines, cross-loaded gullies and lee slopes.

Persistent weak layers

Facets, depth hoar and buried surface hoar can remain reactive long after the storm that created them. They often demand larger terrain margins.

Crusts & interfaces

Rain or melt-freeze crusts may become sliding surfaces, or promote faceting above/below them. Their effect depends on the surrounding layers.

Spatial variability

A pit describes one small place. Wind, aspect, elevation and terrain shape can change the snowpack over very short distances.

How to read a simple snow profile

  1. Choose a representative, safe location—not an exposed start zone.
  2. Identify layer boundaries by hardness/hand resistance and visible grain changes.
  3. Note grain type and size, moisture, crusts and suspected weak layers.
  4. Relate each layer to recent weather: storm snow, wind transport, warming, rain or prolonged cold.
  5. Use stability tests only as one observation alongside the BPA, weather, terrain and red flags.
Important: a stable-looking pit or a non-result does not prove surrounding avalanche terrain is safe.

Basic snow stability tests

Tests can help identify a weak layer and how a fracture behaves at one location. They are observations—not green lights.

Compression Test (CT)

Useful for identifying layers that fracture under incremental loading and for observing fracture character. It says less about whether a fracture will propagate across a slope.

Extended Column Test (ECT)

Designed to add information about fracture propagation across a wider column. Propagating results can be an important warning when the tested layer is representative.

Propagation Saw Test (PST)

Often used for persistent weak layers to investigate whether an initiated crack can continue propagating. Interpretation needs training and careful site selection.

Hand shear / quick checks

Small informal tests can help compare storm interfaces while travelling, but they sample very little snow and should not be treated as a slope-stability verdict.

Good test habits

  • Test in a safe location with similar elevation/aspect/loading to the terrain of interest.
  • Record depth, layer, result and fracture character—not just “passed/failed.”
  • Repeat observations through the day if terrain or weather changes.
  • Give strong warning signs more weight than reassuring isolated tests.

Terrain & decision making

Slope angle

Most slab avalanche terrain is steep enough to slide. Measure or estimate slope angle, but remember you can trigger connected steeper terrain from flatter ground.

Terrain traps

Gullies, creek beds, cliffs, trees and depressions magnify consequences by increasing burial depth or adding trauma.

Runout & overhead hazard

You can be exposed while standing on low-angle terrain beneath a start zone. Ask what is above you as well as what is under your skis.

Group management

Expose one person at a time where appropriate, regroup in genuinely safe locations, maintain communication and avoid stacking the group in a single hazard zone.

Decision traps to watch

  • Commitment to the original objective after conditions change
  • Following tracks or another group as evidence of safety
  • Expert halo: letting confidence in one person replace group observation
  • Scarcity/summit fever: accepting more exposure because the opportunity feels rare
  • Normalisation: “we crossed something like this before”
A useful rule: when uncertainty increases, choose terrain with lower consequences rather than demanding more certainty from imperfect information.

How to read an avalanche bulletin (BPA/BERA)

The European danger level is regional. It is not a rating for every individual slope. Read the detail behind the number.

1. Danger level

Use the 1–5 EAWS scale as the regional starting point and note if the level changes by elevation or time of day.

2. Avalanche problems

Identify the problem type: new snow, wind slab, persistent weak layer, wet snow or gliding snow, and understand what creates it.

3. Where

Check highlighted aspects and elevation bands. Treat boundaries as transitions, not precise lines in the terrain.

4. Likelihood & size

Consider how easy triggering may be, how widespread the problem is and the expected avalanche size/consequence.

5. Trend

Read expected changes from snowfall, wind, warming, cooling or rain. A morning assessment may not describe the afternoon.

6. Verify in the field

Look for evidence that agrees—or conflicts—with the forecast. Unexpected red flags should make you more conservative.

Basic equipment

📡

Transceiver

Wear it securely, verify transmit/search function and battery before departure, and practise searches regularly.

📏

Probe

Carry a proper avalanche probe and know how to deploy it quickly. Probing converts the fine-search signal into a confirmed burial location.

⛏️

Metal shovel

Efficient strategic shovelling is usually the longest part of companion rescue. A robust metal blade matters.

🎒

Avalanche airbag

An airbag can improve survival odds in some avalanches but does not prevent trauma, burial in terrain traps, or the need for rescue gear and terrain judgement.

🩹

First aid & insulation

Carry enough first-aid and insulation to manage trauma, cold and a delayed evacuation.

📱

Communication & navigation

Phone, offline map/GPS and backup power help summon rescue and communicate location, but coverage cannot be assumed.

Equipment is not a permission slip. Rescue tools reduce consequences after a mistake; terrain selection is what reduces exposure before one.

Companion rescue: the sequence to practise

  1. Stop and assess. Make sure rescuers are not entering continuing avalanche danger.
  2. Identify last-seen point and number missing. Shout, look for surface clues and account for the group.
  3. Call 112 early when possible. Give location, number of people, burial status, injuries and access information.
  4. Switch non-searching transceivers to search/off as appropriate. Begin signal search from the last-seen point or systematic search area.
  5. Coarse then fine search. Follow the transceiver to the minimum distance reading close to the snow surface.
  6. Probe systematically. Leave the probe in place when you get a positive strike.
  7. Shovel strategically. Start downhill from the probe and move snow efficiently as a team.
  8. Airway, breathing, trauma, hypothermia. Expose the face/airway first, then manage the patient and prepare for evacuation.
Practise before you need it. Reading this sequence is not rescue competence. Timed transceiver, probing and shovelling practice should be repeated every season.

Main rescue contacts across the Pyrenees

112 works across the European Union and is the best default when you are unsure which specialist service covers the incident.

🇪🇸 Catalonia

112

Emergency coordination dispatches the appropriate mountain-rescue resources, including Bombers de la Generalitat/GRAE where applicable.

🏔️ Val d'Aran

112

Use 112 for mountain incidents; emergency coordination can activate Pompièrs d'Aran and other required resources.

🇪🇸 Aragón / Navarra / Spain

112

Regional emergency coordination dispatches Guardia Civil mountain rescue and medical/fire resources as required.

🇦🇩 Andorra

112

Mountain rescue/emergency coordination. Andorran official emergency contacts also list 118 for firefighters and 116 for medical emergencies.

🇫🇷 French Pyrenees

112

European emergency number. France also uses 18 for fire/rescue and 17 for police/gendarmerie; mountain rescue is dispatched according to location.

Information to give rescuers

  • Exact coordinates and mountain/route name
  • What happened and whether avalanche danger remains
  • Number caught, buried, injured and accounted for
  • Time of avalanche/burial and transceiver status
  • Weather, visibility and safe access/landing information if known
  • Your callback number and remaining group resources

ABOUT THE PROJECT

Built by friends who spend winter in the mountains

We are a small group of friends who ski tour and explore the mountains in winter. We started this project because avalanche information in Europe is excellent in many individual regions, but it is fragmented across borders, languages, bulletin formats and websites.

Our goal is simple: make it easier to find and understand the information that already exists, combine official warnings with clearly labelled community observations, and help people make more informed decisions before and during a day in the mountains.

Safer does not mean more accessible

We do not want this map to make serious winter terrain look easy or consequence-free. Better information should support better judgement, not lower the threshold for entering terrain beyond someone's skills or experience.

Official sources come first

The regional avalanche service remains the authority. Community reports and model weather are additional context and must never replace the official bulletin, field observations or professional advice.

Uncertainty should stay visible

If a current danger rating is unavailable, the map should say so rather than invent one or silently reuse yesterday's value. The same principle applies to community data: useful does not mean verified.

A European direction

The Pyrenees are the first focus because they cross several warning systems in a compact mountain chain. The architecture is deliberately based on European warning-region standards so the Alps and other European ranges can be added later.

Why now?

More people are spending time outdoors in winter. That makes good education, transparent uncertainty, cross-border information and rescue awareness increasingly valuable. The project is a citizen initiative—not an avalanche forecasting centre, guiding service, rescue organisation or guarantee of safety.

Always check the current official bulletin, carry rescue equipment, train with it, and choose terrain appropriate to the conditions and your group.
